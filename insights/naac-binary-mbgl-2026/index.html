NAAC changed the rules: what binary accreditation and MBGL mean for your IQAC

NAAC is transitioning from letter grades toward binary basic accreditation. The basic portal is live, while maturity-level design and technology-supported verification details remain subject to current official manuals. IQAC preparation should therefore focus on evidence integrity without assuming every proposed feature is already operational.

By Dr. Jaskirat Singh · Updated 4 August 2026 · 6 min read

On this page

  1. What actually replaced the grade
  2. The change that will catch institutions off guard
  3. What an IQAC should do now
  4. A tool to start with, today

Following the recommendations of the committee chaired by Dr. K. Radhakrishnan, India’s accreditation reform direction includes broader coverage, binary basic accreditation and a maturity-based framework. Implementation is phased. A live basic-accreditation portal confirms operational movement, but an institution must check its current cycle and manual before assuming that the earlier graded route no longer applies. For the fuller evidence-readiness discussion, see our NAAC reforms guide for IQAC teams.

What actually replaced the grade

The reform direction separates a binary basic-accreditation threshold from later maturity-based differentiation. The live basic portal supports an Accredited / Not Accredited model, but the assessment method and evidence requirements must be read from the current official manual. Ministry of Education material describes expanding accreditation coverage and a gradual shift toward binary accreditation; it does not justify treating every proposed implementation detail as settled.

Maturity-Based Graded Levels are intended to provide developmental differentiation beyond a binary threshold. Secondary materials commonly describe five levels, but exact labels, sequencing, eligibility and benchmarks should be treated as provisional until confirmed in the current NAAC manual. IQAC teams can plan for evidence maturity without presenting a secondary summary as the operative rule.

The change that will catch institutions off guard

Official reform discussions emphasise greater technology-supported verification and better integration of higher-education data. The exact role of AI, the One Nation One Data architecture and automated cross-checking against AISHE or NIRF must be confirmed from current operational documentation. Even where a particular automated check is not yet live, reconciling institutional figures across official submissions is sound evidence governance.

For an honest institution this is good news, because it rewards exactly the thing that is hardest to fake: continuous, consistent, well-kept records. For an institution that has treated accreditation as a periodic documentation sprint, it is a warning. The work shifts from producing a beautiful report every five years to maintaining a defensible data trail every day — the same discipline, incidentally, that good research documentation demands.

What an IQAC should do now

The practical priorities follow directly from the design. Reconcile your data sources first: your AISHE, NIRF and internal figures on enrolment, faculty, research output and finances must tell one coherent story, because official systems may compare or reuse related records. Build the documentation architecture as a living system rather than a filing exercise, so that evidence for each criterion accumulates continuously and is traceable to its source. And be strategic about the two tiers — decide deliberately whether your near-term goal is to secure the binary accreditation cleanly or to make a credible case for a specific maturity level, because the evidence you gather differs depending on which you are aiming at.

Research and its documentation deserve particular attention, because it is the criterion where claims are easiest to inflate and, under stronger data verification, easier to challenge. Publication counts that do not reconcile with indexed records, or that lean on journals whose legitimacy will not survive scrutiny, are now a liability rather than a padding. This is where the discipline we bring to peer-reviewed work — verifiable outputs, honest metrics, defensible records — maps directly onto accreditation readiness.

A tool to start with, today

To inventory records without inventing an accreditation score, our free Institutional Evidence Readiness diagnostic organises twenty self-reported items around the legacy RAF seven-criteria structure and returns present, partial and missing evidence counts. It is explicitly not a BAF/MBGL instrument and does not predict an accreditation outcome.
